## The problem

I know how frustrating it is when digital products don't consider the different ways people think, focus and process information. When an interface feels cluttered or hard to follow, even a simple task starts to feel overwhelming.

For people with ADHD, dyslexia or cognitive overload, dense layouts, unclear hierarchy and too many choices create stress instead of clarity. I wanted to explore accessibility beyond technical standards and design something that feels calmer and easier to move through.

## What the research said

- Cluttered layouts, dense interfaces and inconsistent navigation make information harder to process.
- Lots of products are still designed around “average” users, even though neurodiverse people may process information differently.
- Clearer hierarchy, simpler layouts and shorter sections can reduce cognitive overload.
- Constant choices, notifications and interruptions can make digital spaces mentally exhausting over time.

Sources: Earth Coaching, UXPA Magazine, “Cognitive Load Optimization in User Interface Design for Info Services”, and “Decision Fatigue in the Digital Era”.

## Design principles

1. **Keep things simple.** I tried not to add features or visuals just because I could.
2. **Structure makes things easier to follow.** Consistent layouts mean you don't have to stop and figure out where things are.
3. **Make things easy to read.** Spacing, hierarchy and readability make the interface less tiring.
4. **Guide attention instead of competing for it.** Hierarchy, spacing and small visual cues lead you through, instead of everything shouting at once.

### Applied example: an account sign-up form

I took a standard sign-up form and looked at how small design choices make it feel easier or more overwhelming. The goal wasn't to change what the form does, just how it feels to get through it.

<Figure
  src="/images/clearspace/form-before.png"
  width={672}
  height={1110}
  alt="Before: a single “Create Account” screen with many fields stacked together, red error text and one Submit button."
  caption="Before: everything on one screen."
/>

What wasn't working in the original:

1. The pure white background feels harsh, and the contrast can be uncomfortable for some people.
2. Labels aren't very clear, so you have to stop and think about what each field wants.
3. Every field shows up at once, with no clear place to start.
4. Errors rely only on red, which doesn't work for everyone, and they don't say how to fix the problem.
5. The consent text is small and tucked at the end, so it's easy to skip.

In the new version, it's a step-by-step flow, so you only focus on one thing at a time. I added visible progress, clearer labels, helper text and real-time feedback, so you always know where you are and what's next.

## Final design

### Typography

I hadn't realised how much type affects readability and focus. I wanted text to feel clear, open and easy to move through.

### Colour

I wanted the palette to feel calm and easy on the eyes while still guiding attention. I skipped overly bright colours so the interface supports the content instead of competing with it.

- **Background #EDF1F7:** a softer off-white, because bright white started to feel harsh after a while.
- **Text primary #1A2B3C:** dark enough to stay readable, softer than pure black.
- **Text secondary #3D5468:** for labels and descriptions, to separate information without feeling heavy.
- **Blue #0074A5:** buttons and important actions. It stands out without taking over.
- **Sage #0B764D:** positive actions and confirmations. Calmer than a bright green.
- **Amber #BE7C1C:** when something needs attention, without feeling stressful.
- **Border #D3DCE8:** really light, so content feels organised without looking busy.
