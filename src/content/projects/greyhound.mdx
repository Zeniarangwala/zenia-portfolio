## The problem

Greyhound has moved people across America since 1914, and its whole promise is cheap long-distance travel. The chrome dog and script wordmark are everywhere, but the brand feels old, grey and “cheap bus”.

## Research

**Who rides.** Mostly young adults (18–24) and seniors (65+) on low to middle incomes who need to travel far for cheap. I turned that into three personas:

- **Aditi, 21, a student** who needs a trip that fits her budget and is easy to figure out.
- **Liam, 28, a solo traveller** who wants the trip to feel worth taking.
- **Mariana, 38, a mom** who needs travelling with her kids to feel simple and dependable.

**The competition.** Megabus is cheaper but has no stations, so you wait on the curb. Peter Pan is cleaner but only on the East Coast. Amtrak is comfortable but costs much more. Every one of them is selling a seat.

**The insight.** Nobody owns the experience of the ride. My SWOT pointed to the same opportunity: make the journey itself part of the product.

## Strategy

| Old story | New story |
|---|---|
| The cheap way to get there | A way to go somewhere |
| Affordable | Accessible |
| Basic | Spontaneous |
| A bus ride | A road trip |

The big idea became **every journey has a story**, built on the American road trip people already daydream about. My first moodboard (sage, burnt orange, maps) felt flat. Open roads, desert, coastline, bus windows at sunset and Route 66 are where it clicked.

## Identity

One rule from the start: **don't change the dog.** It was the most recognisable thing Greyhound had.

### Logo: three tries

1. **Chrome dog on sunsets.** Still looked like the old brand.
2. **Gradient dog.** The dog in a blue-to-orange sunset gradient, with a blue wordmark.
3. **Final.** A cream dog and new wordmark on Majestic Mountain Blue. Simpler, warmer, and it works on everything, including the dog on its own as an icon.

## System

### From generic to regional

My first pattern was soft leaf shadows. They were pretty, but they could belong to any brand. Critique pushed me toward symbols that tell you where you are: peaches for Georgia, boots and cacti for Texas, palms for the coast.

## Iteration: the ticket, three times

My first round of components (a road card, a stamp card, a boarding pass and an Instagram post) looked like an airline. Critique gave me five notes that shaped everything after:

- **It's a bus, not a plane.** No gates, no boarding pass.
- **Make the printed ticket real:** black and white, letter size.
- **Fewer words**, no fake drop shadows.
- **Put the brand voice** in everything.
- **Design for Mariana too**, the mom travelling with kids.

The ticket shows that shift most clearly.

## Takeaways

The line I landed on: **the journey is part of the destination.**

- **Keep what works.** Leaving the dog alone made every other change easier to trust.
- **Specific beats pretty.** Leaf shadows were nice. Peaches, boots and palm trees tell you where you are.
- **Critique is a design tool.** The paper ticket, the photo overlays, the brand voice and the colouring books all came out of feedback.

**Next, I'd** put the printed ticket and the Window Seat Guide in front of real riders at a station, to see whether the road-trip story lands with people who just need a cheap seat.
